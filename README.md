# AI-Standards-Meeting-Note
<h2>Debrief on Oct 28, 2026 AI Standards Event, Building Trustworthy AI</h2>

<b> Overall Takeaways:</b>
- The importance of having humans in control of AI technology to demonstrate its economic potential
- Standards are used as a self-improvement exercise for companies to build systems that are trusted
- Singapore’s National AI Strategy is more advanced than Canada’s, with the goal of maximizing societal and economic benefits while considering three level of risks including existential risks 
<p></p>
Keynote speakers raised key questions about building trustworthy AI

- How to get the standards and research community to work together without impeding innovation?
- Red teaming and evaluation in independent organizations are strong; the AI Safety Institute in the UK was highlighted. However, frontier AI companies might not publish their research findings with the scientific community; there is an inability to create a productive environment to do this work well.
<p>
AI Infrastructure session showcased the current paths organizations take to mitigate AI risks and build trust</p>

- For large organizations: No rapid gain from AI after 4 years and getting this right is important. Trust is the key challenge. The current strategy focused on delivering relentless care in three areas: 1) Human-centric engineering, 2) Data and AI literacy, 3) Communication on being trustworthy
- For small and medium-sized businesses: cybersecurity events will be more frequent; businesses need to differentiate between we don’t trust it and we don’t comprehend it. Certification of AI tools will provide reassurance and trust needs to be demonstrated. There is also a shift in consumer protection where companies are liable for information they provide, whether by human or chatbot (see Moffat v. Air Canada lawsuit)
- For small AI developers: there is a need to set the right mindset and intention to build a trusted system in the software development cycle. This includes 1) define the domain in which the system will operate in; 2) follow ethical principles; 3) operate within the legal framework of that domain. Guardrails should be developed from the bottom up. 

<p>Risk Management session explored three themes</p>

<i>1. Sovereignty and international cooperation</i>

- AI is important for national security and economic development. Sovereignty is not solitude. Need to maintain agency and not tackle everything. 
- Find common meaning where we can diverge
- Honour the Crown in the nation-to-nation relationship

<i>2. Trust and opportunities</i>

- Trust in AI in Canada is low, and trust is the foundation and premise for adoption. 
- Bill C36 and the AI Transparency Consultation focused on creating confidence and trust for adoption. CAISI is building a pool of talent to tackle AI safety and evaluation.
- Technology makes things convenient, and in the accessibility context, technology makes things possible. This relationship with technology makes us vulnerable; we need to be careful of digital eugenics when algorithms push us into monoculture and accessibility needs are not considered in the statistics gathered.
- Respect Indigenous data sovereignty through IM/IT/Information Governance practices

<i>3. Governance, standards, and accountability</i>

- AI for All strategy highlighted National AI literacy to raise literacy across all. Bill C8, 16 and 34 are addressing AI risks. Incident tracking was part of the AI Transparency Consultation. Canada's trusted certification program is under development.
- Information collection from First Nations should follow Indigenous data sovereignty principles.
- Vulnerable groups are outliers in the current risk impact assessment, and marginalized groups are already bartering their privacy for services; current protection is not in the personal interests of the majority, and voluntary standards are not enough. 

<p>AI Adoption Journey focused on ways to build AI assurance and the elements suggested by practitioners include independent evaluation, certification, open-source standards, and internationally recognized frameworks. </p>

- Best practice:
  
  - Build a multidisciplinary team for effective governance 
  - Give clear ownership of evaluation, implementation and assessment
  - Governance needs to be dynamic 
  - Need a code-level certification
  - Deployer and developer have shared responsibility

- Current gaps:
  
  - Risk-based standards and the need to remain flexible and relevant to keep up the pace of technology as standards are being built out
  - More templates and guidelines to capture the early adopters and emerging market
  - Adversarial actors don’t care about standards.
